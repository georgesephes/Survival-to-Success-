What Lens Are You Looking Through?

How we see God, ourselves, other people, and our circumstances shapes how we respond to life.

Survival to Success • By George Walley-Sephes

Two people can experience the same event and walk away with completely different conclusions. One sees rejection; another sees redirection. One sees failure; another sees information. One assumes silence means abandonment; another experiences the same silence as an invitation to wait, reflect, or change direction.

The event matters. But so does the lens through which we interpret it.

We do not respond only to reality

We respond to our interpretation of reality.

That interpretation is shaped by experience, relationships, trauma, culture, expectations, fear, faith, memory, and the stories we have learned to tell about ourselves. Over time, those stories can become so familiar that we stop recognizing them as interpretations and begin treating them as facts.

This is where distorted thinking can become especially powerful.

When the lens is distorted, even accurate information can be interpreted in a way that reinforces survival.

Four important lenses

Much of life can be understood through four connected perspectives: how we see God, ourselves, other people, and our circumstances.

If I believe God is distant, I may interpret waiting as abandonment. If I believe I am incapable, I may interpret challenge as proof that I should quit. If I expect other people to reject me, I may interpret neutral behavior as hostility. If I believe my circumstances determine my future, temporary difficulty can begin to feel permanent.

The problem is not simply that negative thoughts feel bad. The problem is that beliefs influence behavior.

Beliefs create patterns

What we believe affects what we notice. What we notice affects what we expect. What we expect affects how we respond. Our responses create consequences, and those consequences can then appear to confirm what we believed in the first place.

This is one way survival becomes self-reinforcing.

A person who believes “nothing ever works for me” may stop taking meaningful risks. The lack of new action produces few new opportunities, and the absence of opportunity then becomes further evidence that nothing works.

Transformation requires a different way of seeing

Changing perspective does not mean denying pain, ignoring injustice, pretending everything is positive, or calling harmful circumstances good. Healthy reframing begins with truth.

It asks: What am I assuming? What evidence do I actually have? What am I afraid this means? Is there another way to interpret this? What does my faith teach me about God, myself, other people, and this situation?

Transformation often begins before circumstances change. It begins when the meaning we assign to those circumstances changes.

A question to sit with

What belief about God, yourself, another person, or your circumstances may be shaping your decisions right now—and what would change if that belief were not entirely true?
